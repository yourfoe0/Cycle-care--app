Notes to each other

The four phases

What's actually happening, hormonally, at each point in a typical cycle.

Days 1–5
Menstrual
The uterine lining sheds. Energy is often lower, cramps and fatigue are common — rest is genuinely useful, not laziness.
Days 1–13
Follicular
Estrogen rises and energy climbs. A good window for starting new tasks or exercise.
Around ovulation
Ovulatory
An egg is released. Energy and mood are typically at their highest point in the cycle.
Days 15–28
Luteal
Progesterone rises then drops. PMS symptoms — mood shifts, bloating, cravings, tiredness — tend to show up late in this phase.
Helping regulate the cycle
Consistent sleep — supports the hormone rhythms that drive the cycle.
Steady, balanced meals — enough protein, iron, and healthy fats.
Moderate, regular movement — very intense or erratic training can disrupt regularity.
Managing stress — chronic stress disrupts cycle-governing hormones.
Hydration — can ease bloating and cramping.
When to see a doctor — cycles consistently under 21 or over 35 days, missed periods, or disruptive pain are worth a medical opinion.
Worth avoiding, as a partner
  • Dismissing pain or saying she's "overreacting."
  • Joking about "being on her period" during disagreements.
  • Pressuring intimacy she's not up for.
  • Complaining about mood changes instead of asking how to help.
Pregnancy basics

This is general education, not medical advice or a diagnostic tool. Every pregnancy is different — an OB/GYN or midwife should always be the first call for anything concerning.

A general overview of the three trimesters.
Weeks 1–13
First trimester
Major organ development happens here. Nausea, fatigue, and heightened smell sensitivity are common. The first prenatal visit usually happens around week 8.
Weeks 14–27
Second trimester
Often the easiest stretch — energy tends to return. Movement is usually felt around weeks 18–20, alongside the anatomy scan.
Weeks 28–40+
Third trimester
Rapid growth, more frequent prenatal visits, and practice contractions (Braxton Hicks) are common as the body prepares for labor.
Supporting a pregnancy — Act & React
Lime — Act
  • Come to prenatal appointments when you can.
  • Learn the foods and habits to avoid together.
  • Take over the physically demanding chores.
  • Keep a running list of questions for the doctor.
Lemon — React
  • If smells or nausea hit hard: don't take it personally, keep crackers nearby.
  • If she raises a symptom: take it seriously rather than reassuring it away.
  • If labor signs start: stay calm — that's the most useful thing you can do.
Warning signs — contact a doctor promptly
  • Heavy vaginal bleeding or fluid leaking
  • Severe or persistent abdominal pain
  • A severe headache that won't go away, especially with vision changes
  • Sudden swelling of the face, hands, or feet
  • Noticeably reduced or absent baby movement (after movement is typically felt)
  • Contractions before 37 weeks, or a high fever
